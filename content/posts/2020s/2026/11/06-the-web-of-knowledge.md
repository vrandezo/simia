I thought I knew the history of Wikipedia inside and out. Then I read
[Giota Alevizou](https://www.kcl.ac.uk/people/giota-alevizou)’s [The Web
of
Knowledge](https://kclpure.kcl.ac.uk/portal/en/publications/the-web-of-knowledge-encyclopedias-and-authority-in-the-digital-a/),
and I found myself honestly surprised. I learned things about Wikipedia
and its role in the knowledge ecosystem that had a major impact on my
career, and that I was completely unaware of.

2026 is Wikipedia’s Jubilee year, and there are a few books published
retelling the history of Wikipedia. But this book goes further; it
discusses the role of Wikipedia in the wider knowledge infrastructure,
and it encompasses the history of (English) digital encyclopedias as a
whole, covering in particular the journeys of Britannica and Encarta
from the 1990s on, in addition to telling the history of Wikipedia.

The book starts with a foreword by Jimmy Wales, the founder of
Wikipedia, in which he closes by discussing the impact AI might have on
Wikipedia and the knowledge ecosystem. A timely topic that the book will
pick up.

I have to admit that I had a bit of a hard time getting started with the
book, particularly regarding its positioning within its theoretical
frameworks. But once we started to dive into the topic, I enjoyed it
quite a bit and learned a few things.

The book covers very competently the pre-history of digital
encyclopedias, starting well before Diderot, covering the ‘Great Books’
of the past, the Summae, and also Otlet and Dewey and their plans to
index and organize the world’s knowledge, and Nelson’s Memex, all the
way to Lenat’s Cyc. Giota describes knowledge organization and the
encyclopedia as parallel threads weaving through the centuries.

For me the biggest surprise was right in the origin story of Wikipedia,
where both threads were tied together. A lot has been written and said
about Jimmy Wales’ motivation to start an online encyclopedia, including
his mother buying an encyclopedia when he was 3 years old. When Jimmy
was 30 years old, in 1996, he founded Bomis, a web portal for
user-generated webrings and adult content. Critics often raised the
second part, but Giota’s book focuses on the first: to structure the
topics of the webrings would be helpful. And what better solution for
that than an encyclopedia?

This was around the same time that Yahoo started becoming the main
portal to the Web, and that predecessors of DMOZ came around. Back then,
Yahoo wasn’t a search portal, but a directory. Jimmy’s intuition that an
encyclopedia would be a great complement or substrate to organize the
webrings would prove extremely foresighted, and became one of the
foundational ideas for my own career.

That’s why I was so surprised to read about it in this book. So much
that I actually had to discuss this with Jimmy and he, unsurprisingly,
confirmed the story.

An encyclopedia is “a systematically organized repository of knowledge”.
Already in the early 20th century, Giota describes how “the encyclopedia
serves as a means for the semantic structuring of (digital) information
spaces”.

I want to expand on this point: Jimmy’s intuition repeatedly bore fruit
in the years that followed. Ironically, to the best of my understanding
he never used it to improve the organization of webrings by Bomis. In
2005, Markus Krötzsch, Max Völkel and I described the [Semantic
Wikipedia](https://iccl.inf.tu-dresden.de/web/Inproceedings963/en) idea,
where we suggested using Wikipedia articles as anchors for topics.

In 2007, Metaweb’s Freebase also took Wikipedia as the central semantic
structure to build around, which later became the Google Knowledge
Graph, considerably improving Google Search by using [“things, not
strings”](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/search/introducing-knowledge-graph-things-not/).
DBpedia, started in the same year, was also lifted off from Wikipedia
and called itself a [Nucleus for the Web of Open
Data](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-540-76298-0_52).

In 2012 [we kicked off
Wikidata](https://iccl.inf.tu-dresden.de/w/images/9/9d/Vrandecic-Pintscher-Kroetzsch_Wikidata-History-WWW-2023.pdf)
to explicitly provide and entangle a machine-readable knowledge graph,
firmly based on Wikipedia. Abstract Wikipedia is now a continuation of
this idea, working toward a more comprehensive knowledge representation
than Wikidata did, towards the more ambitious goal of allowing many more
people to share knowledge across language barriers.

And although I have been working on this for more than 20 years, I did
not know that using an encyclopedia as the foundational structure of the
semantic space was Jimmy’s motivation all along.

But back to the book. For me, that insight alone was worth the prize of
admission. And Giota doesn’t stop there. There is the widespread myth
that *“Wikipedia killed Britannica”*, and maybe some of us know that
this doesn’t align with events. Britannica was already in a steep
decline when Wikipedia was founded, and the reason was Microsoft’s
Encarta. And whereas I roughly knew this story, Giota really lays out
the narrative with thorough evidence drawn from her own material
collected over decades. She had already interviewed people involved with
Britannica and Encarta in the 2000s, and used this material to lay out a
fascinating story, about Britannica’s turbulent organizational history
and Microsoft’s ambitions to use Encarta as the promotional vehicle to
get a PC into every household: because for the price of a Britannica,
you not only get an encyclopedia for your children, but you get a lot
more value.

One interesting point is about finances: Giota mentions that in 1989,
Britannica had $627 million in revenue. That’s almost [$1.6 billion in
today’s
dollars](https://www.wikifunctions.org/wiki/Special:RunFunction?call=%7B%22Z1K1%22%3A%22Z7%22%2C%22Z7K1%22%3A%22Z25445%22%2C%22Z25445K1%22%3A%7B%22Z1K1%22%3A%22Z7%22%2C%22Z7K1%22%3A%22Z25970%22%2C%22Z25970K1%22%3A%7B%22Z1K1%22%3A%22Z19677%22%2C%22Z19677K1%22%3A%7B%22Z1K1%22%3A%22Z16659%22%2C%22Z16659K1%22%3A%22Z16660%22%7D%2C%22Z19677K2%22%3A%7B%22Z1K1%22%3A%22Z13518%22%2C%22Z13518K1%22%3A%22627000000%22%7D%2C%22Z19677K3%22%3A%7B%22Z1K1%22%3A%22Z13518%22%2C%22Z13518K1%22%3A%221%22%7D%7D%2C%22Z25970K2%22%3A%7B%22Z1K1%22%3A%22Z20159%22%2C%22Z20159K1%22%3A%7B%22Z1K1%22%3A%22Z17813%22%2C%22Z17813K1%22%3A%22Z17814%22%7D%2C%22Z20159K2%22%3A%7B%22Z1K1%22%3A%22Z13518%22%2C%22Z13518K1%22%3A%221989%22%7D%7D%2C%22Z25970K3%22%3A%7B%22Z1K1%22%3A%22Z20159%22%2C%22Z20159K1%22%3A%7B%22Z1K1%22%3A%22Z17813%22%2C%22Z17813K1%22%3A%22Z17814%22%7D%2C%22Z20159K2%22%3A%7B%22Z1K1%22%3A%22Z13518%22%2C%22Z13518K1%22%3A%222026%22%7D%7D%7D%2C%22Z25445K2%22%3A%7B%22Z1K1%22%3A%22Z13518%22%2C%22Z13518K1%22%3A%222%22%7D%7D).

That is roughly one order of magnitude larger than the budget of the
Wikimedia Foundation. While Britannica is available only in English,
Wikipedia is available in more than 300 languages.

Giota also reminds us that Encarta premiered multimedia capabilities and
functionalities from which Wikipedia could still learn. But just as
Encarta eroded Britannica’s financial base, Wikipedia was able to
replace Encarta. Microsoft wouldn’t even put up a fight: the PC had
already won the marketplace, and whether Encarta or Wikipedia was the
encyclopedia, what did it matter to Microsoft?

The book outlines the history of how encyclopedic authority moved from a
small group of exalted individuals to a crowd in which everyone can
participate. It reminds us again how littered with accidents this
history was, and how fragile the current situation is. The wide
deployment and usage of AI is destabilizing a situation in more than one
way, and the book outlines what we may lose if we are not careful. We
must take our role as stewards of the encyclopedia seriously – I am
reminded of Umberto Eco’s wonderful quote: "After all, the cultivated
person's first duty is to be always prepared to rewrite the
encyclopaedia."
